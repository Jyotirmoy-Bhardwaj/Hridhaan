Manifesto

The reading problem is solved. The evidence problem is not.

Every few months a new model reads technical documents better than the last. That capability is not scarce, not defensible, and within a release or two it will be free. Building a company on reading slightly better than a general assistant is building on somebody else's roadmap.

What does not arrive with the next model is the ability to prove how a decision was made. A regulated decision has a different shape from a good answer. Reproducible — the same document, the same rulebook, the same finding in three years. Traceable to the dated version of the rule in force that day. Quoting the passage rather than paraphrasing it. And signed by someone who can be named when an inspector asks.

No general assistant offers this, and none will — closing that gap would mean freezing model versions on a customer's behalf. This is not a gap in their product. It is a consequence of it.

So the work that survives is unglamorous. Rule packs with dates attached. Evaluation sets labelled by an organisation's own experts. Validation documentation generated by the release. Write-back under change control, with an e-signature and an audit trail. None of that is intelligence. All of it is labour — and labour is not what model releases erode.

The interesting question was never how well the machine reads. It is who signs, what they are signing, and whether the reasoning still stands years after everyone involved has moved on.

Model releases commoditise intelligence. They do not commoditise evidence.

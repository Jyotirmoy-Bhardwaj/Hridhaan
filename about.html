Manifesto

The reading problem is solved. The evidence problem is not.

Every few months a new model reads technical documents better than the last one. That capability is not scarce, it is not defensible, and within a release or two it will be free. Building a company on reading documents slightly better than a general assistant is building on somebody else's roadmap.

What does not arrive with the next model is the ability to prove how a decision was made.

A regulated decision has a different shape from a good answer. It has to be reproducible — the same document, against the same rulebook, returning the same finding in three years, or a documented reason why not. It has to be traceable to the dated version of a rule that was in force on the day. It has to quote the passage it relied on rather than paraphrase it. And it has to be signed by someone who can be named when an inspector asks.

No general assistant offers this, and none will. Closing that gap would mean freezing model versions on a customer's behalf, which is the opposite of how those businesses are built. This is not a gap in their product. It is a consequence of it.

So the work that survives is unglamorous. Rule packs with dates attached. Evaluation sets labelled by an organisation's own experts, against its own historic decisions. Validation documentation generated by the release rather than written afterwards by hand. Write-back into a system of record under change control, with an e-signature and an audit trail. None of that is intelligence. All of it is labour — and labour is not what model releases erode.

The interesting question in regulated AI was never how well the machine reads. It is who signs, what exactly they are signing, and whether the reasoning still stands up years after everyone involved has moved on.

Model releases commoditise intelligence. They do not commoditise evidence.
